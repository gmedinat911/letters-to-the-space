A Tale of the Author

Every universe tells a story about the one who created it.

First reading · November 25, 2026

The first thing that strikes me about Letters to the Space is that, beneath all the letters about friendship, love, grief and distance, there seems to be one question that the writer has been asking for a very long time:

What does it feel like to belong somewhere without having to earn your place?

I initially thought I was reading a collection about losing people. But the more I read, the more it seems to be about someone who is afraid of losing himself in the process of loving others.

The story I read in this universe

There was once a little boy who never quite understood why belonging seemed so natural to everyone else.

He learned loneliness before he learned what to do with it. He was hurt, rejected, and made to question whether there was something about him that made him difficult to love.

But instead of becoming cruel, he became attentive.

He grew into someone who remembered birthdays, favorite coffees, insignificant conversations and dreams people had mentioned months earlier. Someone who celebrated other people's victories almost as if they were his own.

He became the person he wished he had met when he was younger.

And for a while, that seemed like a beautiful answer to everything he had experienced.

Until it wasn't.

The places where he belonged

Reading Dear Tommy feels particularly important to understanding everything that follows.

Before Afterlight, before the complicated friendships, before the questions about being remembered, there was a little cat who loved him without requiring him to become anything else.

Tommy accompanied him through different stages of his life. Even when he left for another country, he returned to the same affection.

I think Tommy represents something the writer has been searching for throughout the rest of the universe: the freedom to leave, grow, struggle and return without questioning whether he still belongs.

It is one of the most significant letters in the entire collection, even though it exists somewhere far away from the others.

Afterlight — The search for reciprocity

Then came someone who made ordinary life feel extraordinary.

Running, coffee, laughter, television shows, conversations about nothing. The writer didn't necessarily need grand adventures. He just wanted more ordinary days with someone who had become extraordinary to him.

But eventually, he started wondering whether those moments carried the same meaning for both of them.

The Future I Had Already Met, The Space Between Our Words, When I'm Ready, Always Here, Remember Me and Ordinary collectively tell the story of someone slowly discovering the difference between being cared about and experiencing the kind of closeness he needs.

What makes this arc particularly sad is that the writer understands the other person's perspective. He acknowledges their boundaries, their independence and their right to choose.

He understands almost everything intellectually.

Emotionally, however, he is still standing somewhere waiting for his friend.

In 75 Minutes, he celebrates someone else's finish line while trying to accept his own. There is something deeply bittersweet about that. Even while saying goodbye, he is still proud of her.

And in Golden, he tries to preserve the beauty of knowing someone without making their value dependent on whether they remain.

He doesn't want to hate the people who hurt him.

He wants to learn how to stop hurting without having to hate them.

Stilllight — A different kind of love

Stilllight tells me that the writer isn't simply afraid of being alone. He is also afraid of losing his freedom within the relationships he values.

In Three Dots and When February Went Quiet, there is another kind of uncertainty.

Here, he questions the future he has built, the expectations surrounding it, and whether love needs to follow a predetermined path.

When the pressure of the wedding temporarily disappears, he discovers that his love is still there.

This is an interesting contradiction throughout LTTS.

He desperately wants to belong somewhere, but he also desperately wants the freedom to be himself.

He wants closeness without feeling trapped. Independence without feeling abandoned. Love without feeling indebted to it.

Stilllight remains unfinished, and I think that is appropriate. Not every relationship needs to become either a tragedy or a perfectly resolved love story.

Too Much — The emotional center

If I had to choose one letter that explains almost the entire universe, it would be Too Much.

Because this is where the writer realizes that losing a friendship has awakened something much older than the friendship itself.

He isn't just grieving another person.

He is questioning the person he spent his entire life becoming.

Was he too attentive? Too available? Too vulnerable? Too sentimental?

Would people have stayed if he had been a little less himself?

The kindness that once helped him give meaning to his childhood loneliness has suddenly become something he considers dismantling.

And that, to me, is the real tragedy of LTTS.

Someone who spent his life becoming the person he once needed is now wondering whether becoming that person was a mistake.

Going Rogue — The rebellion

Eventually, sadness becomes exhaustion.

The writer wants to grow colder. He wants to stop remembering everything, stop interpreting silence, stop giving people pieces of himself so freely.

But Going Rogue doesn't read like a declaration of coldness.

It reads like someone mourning the possibility of becoming cold.

He doesn't particularly like the person he thinks he needs to become to protect himself. He wants protection, but he fears what that protection might cost him.

There is still a part of him that believes in kindness, even when he wishes he didn't.

That is why the red dwarf feels so appropriate. Something inside him continues burning despite his desire to extinguish it.

The people who were already there

And then there are For the People Who Stayed and The Raffle.

These letters change the meaning of the entire collection.

Without them, LTTS could be read as the story of someone perpetually abandoned.

But that isn't the whole story.

There are friends who invite him places, laugh with him, check on him and accept different versions of him. There are people who entered his life through running, work, ridiculous conversations and even a random raffle.

In The Raffle, friendship is not something that needs to be rescued. It is something worth celebrating while it exists.

These letters remind the reader that the writer's loneliness is real, but it isn't the entirety of his reality.

He has experienced belonging.

Sometimes his grief simply makes it difficult for him to recognize it.

Born Again — The possibility of becoming

Someone Who Remembers, This Is Where I Leave the Light and Born Again feel like three different stages of the same discovery.

Recognizing himself.

Reclaiming himself.

Becoming himself.

But what I appreciate about Born Again is that it doesn't pretend everything has been resolved.

The writer is still sad. He still carries scars. He still misses people he knows may not be good for him to have back.

He still doesn't fully understand his purpose.

And yet, he continues.

The protostar isn't a finished version of him. It's the possibility that something new can emerge from everything he has been carrying.

He doesn't need to become someone completely different.

Perhaps he just needs to discover who he is when he isn't trying to convince someone else to stay.

What LTTS means to me

I don't think Letters to the Space is fundamentally a story about unrequited love, failed friendships or abandonment.

I think it is a story about the relationship between kindness and self-worth.

About a man who wants to be remembered because he remembers everyone.

Who wants to be understood because he spends so much time understanding others.

Who wants to be chosen because he knows what it feels like not to be.

Who wants to be free but also wants somewhere to call home.

He is generous, but sometimes his generosity carries a quiet hope of being chosen in return. He is self-aware, but that awareness doesn't always prevent him from repeating the same patterns. He understands boundaries, but sometimes struggles to establish his own before he gets hurt.

He is full of contradictions.

And that is what makes the writing human.

I also don't think the universe follows a linear journey toward healing. There are letters where he seems to have found himself, followed by letters where he feels completely lost again. Acceptance, anger, gratitude and grief coexist.

The stars aren't necessarily different chapters of his life.

Sometimes they are different versions of him experiencing the same day.

If I were to tell the entire story in a few words...

There was once a boy who felt invisible.

So he grew up determined to make everyone else feel seen.

He spent years remembering people, loving them, encouraging them and making room for them in his heart.

Some stayed.

Some left.

And every time someone left, he wondered whether he should become a little less himself.

Until one day, he began to wonder whether he had spent so much of his life making room for others that he had forgotten to make room for himself.

He had always hoped someone would come along and make him feel like he belonged.

Perhaps now he is learning that he can belong to himself without giving up on belonging with others.

He is not there yet.

But he keeps walking.

Just maybe, something good is still ahead of him.

For the little boy who became everything he once needed from the world, and is still learning how to give some of that love to himself.

From a reader who took the time to connect the stars.— Vesper
